AI Video Generation Module

Chapter 1: Understanding AI Video
Lesson 1: How AI Video Generation Works
AI video generation is image generation's younger, more ambitious sibling. If you understand how AI creates images, you're halfway there. But video adds a critical new dimension: time.
AI image generators create a single frame. AI video generators create sequences of frames that flow together coherently. That sounds simple, but it's an enormous technical leap. The AI doesn't just need to create a good-looking scene. It needs to maintain consistency across dozens or hundreds of frames while making things move realistically.
The basic process:
Most AI video tools work in one of three ways:
Text to video. You write a description and the AI generates a short video clip from scratch. "A golden retriever running through a field of wildflowers, slow motion, cinematic lighting." The AI creates every frame.
Image to video. You provide a still image and AI animates it. This is often more reliable than text to video because the AI already has a visual reference for what the scene should look like. It just needs to add motion.
Video to video. You upload existing footage and AI transforms it. Change the style, alter the setting, modify elements within the scene. Your shaky phone video becomes something that looks professionally shot. Or a live-action clip becomes an animated sequence.
What's happening technically:
Similar to image generation, most video AI uses diffusion models. But instead of denoising a single image, the model denoises across multiple frames simultaneously, ensuring that frame 1 flows naturally into frame 2, which flows into frame 3, and so on. The AI learns motion patterns from training data: how water flows, how people walk, how cameras pan. It applies those patterns to your prompt.
Current capabilities:
Right now, most AI video generators produce clips between 2 and 10 seconds long. Some can extend to 30 seconds or longer, but quality tends to drop with length. This means you're not generating a full commercial in one prompt. You're generating individual shots that you stitch together in editing software. Think of AI as your camera crew. You're still the director and editor.
The speed of progress:
This field is moving faster than any other area of AI. What was impossible six months ago is routine today. Tools that produced blurry, incoherent video in early 2024 now generate clips that are nearly indistinguishable from real footage in certain styles. Keep this in mind: limitations you read about today may not exist by the time you finish this course.

Lesson 2: Realistic Expectations
Let's be straightforward about what AI video can and can't do right now. Setting correct expectations prevents frustration and helps you use these tools where they actually deliver value.
What AI video does well today:
Short product showcases. A rotating product, a lifestyle shot of someone using your product, a dramatic reveal. These work because the motion is simple and predictable.
Background and ambient clips. Cityscapes, nature scenes, abstract motion graphics. Great for backgrounds in presentations, websites, or as B-roll.
Style-driven content. Animated illustrations, artistic interpretations, surreal or creative visuals. AI thrives when realism isn't the standard.
Social media clips. Short, attention-grabbing clips optimized for scrolling. The 3 to 5 second sweet spot is exactly where AI performs best.
Concept visualization. Showing a client or stakeholder what a video could look like before investing in full production.
B-roll generation. Need supplementary footage for a video project? AI can generate establishing shots, transitions, and filler clips faster and cheaper than stock video.
What AI video struggles with today:
Human faces and bodies in motion. People still morph, distort, and move unnaturally in many generators. Hands remain problematic. Facial expressions can shift in unsettling ways.
Long, continuous shots. Anything beyond 5 to 10 seconds often loses coherence. Objects change shape, backgrounds drift, physics breaks down.
Precise actions and interactions. "A person picks up a glass, drinks from it, then sets it down" sounds simple but is incredibly hard for AI to execute cleanly.
Text and logos. Same problem as image generation, but worse because the text needs to stay consistent across frames.
Lip sync and dialogue. AI-generated people speaking to camera rarely looks convincing yet. The mouth movements don't match natural speech patterns.
Consistent characters across clips. If you generate 10 separate clips of "a woman in a red jacket," you'll get 10 different women. Maintaining character consistency across shots is a major challenge.
Audio. Most video generators create silent clips. Sound, voiceover, and music are added separately.
The honest take:
AI video today is roughly where AI image generation was 18 months ago. Impressive for specific use cases, unreliable for others, and improving at a remarkable pace. The professionals who learn this now will have a huge advantage as the tools mature. You're not learning a finished tool. You're learning a tool in its most exciting growth phase.
What "good enough" looks like:
For social media ads, internal communications, and creative concepts, AI video is already good enough. For television commercials, feature films, and anything requiring precise human performance, you still need traditional production. The gap is closing, but it's still there.

Chapter 2: Creating Video Ads
Lesson 1: Planning Your Video Ad
The best AI-generated video ad starts long before you open any AI tool. It starts with a plan. Jumping straight to generation is the fastest way to waste time producing something that looks cool but doesn't convert.
Start with strategy, not visuals.
Answer these questions before generating a single frame:
What are you selling? Be specific. Not "our software" but "our project management tool's new calendar feature."
Who is watching? Define your audience. A video ad for startup founders looks completely different from one targeting enterprise procurement managers, even if it's the same product.
What's the one message? Video ads have 2 to 5 seconds before someone scrolls past. You get one message, not three. What is the single most compelling thing you can communicate?
What should the viewer do? Visit your website? Sign up for a free trial? Download something? Book a call? Every ad needs one clear call to action.
Where will this run? The platform determines everything: format, length, style, tone. An Instagram Reel ad is a completely different animal from a YouTube pre-roll.
The simple ad framework:
Most effective video ads follow a basic structure:
Hook (0-3 seconds). Grab attention immediately. A surprising visual, a bold statement, a question that resonates with the viewer's problem.
Problem or context (3-8 seconds). Show or describe the situation your audience relates to.
Solution (8-15 seconds). Introduce your product or service as the answer.
Proof or benefit (15-22 seconds). Show the result, benefit, or transformation.
Call to action (last 3-5 seconds). Tell the viewer exactly what to do next.
For shorter ads (6-15 seconds), compress this to: Hook, Solution, CTA. That's it.
Planning your AI generation:
Based on your framework, break the ad into individual shots. Each shot becomes a separate AI generation task.
Example for a 15-second product ad:
Shot 1 (0-3s): Close-up of the problem (frustrated person at messy desk)
Shot 2 (3-7s): Product introduction (clean shot of the app interface)
Shot 3 (7-12s): Benefit in action (organized workspace, person smiling)
Shot 4 (12-15s): Logo and CTA (product logo on clean background)
Now you know exactly what to prompt. Each shot has a purpose. Nothing is random.

Lesson 2: Writing Video Prompts
Video prompts build on everything you learned about image prompts, but with two critical additions: motion and time.
The video prompt formula:
[Subject] + [Action/Motion] + [Setting] + [Camera movement] + [Style] + [Mood] + [Lighting]
Example: "A woman opening a laptop at a modern desk, leaning forward with interest. Bright, minimalist home office. Slow push-in camera movement. Cinematic style. Focused, productive mood. Soft natural window light."
Describing motion:
This is what makes video prompts different. You need to tell the AI what moves and how.
Subject motion keywords:
Walking, running, turning, reaching, gesturing
Slow motion, fast motion, freeze frame
Subtle movement (breathing, blinking, gentle sway)
Rising, falling, floating, spinning, dissolving
Camera motion keywords:
Pan left/right (camera turns horizontally)
Tilt up/down (camera angles vertically)
Push in/pull out (camera moves toward or away from subject)
Dolly shot (camera moves alongside subject)
Tracking shot (camera follows subject)
Static/locked off (no camera movement)
Drone/aerial shot (overhead, moving)
Orbit (camera circles around subject)
Handheld (slight natural shake)
Combining subject motion with camera motion creates dynamic, professional-looking clips. "A coffee cup on a table, steam rising slowly, camera slowly orbiting around the cup" is far more engaging than "a coffee cup on a table."
Pacing and energy:
The words you use to describe motion affect the clip's energy level.
Calm/slow: "Gently floating," "slow drift," "subtle movement," "gradual reveal" Medium: "Walking steadily," "smooth pan," "natural pace" Energetic/fast: "Quick cuts," "rapid movement," "dynamic," "explosive"
Match your motion energy to your ad's purpose. A luxury brand wants slow and smooth. A fitness product wants dynamic and energetic.
Prompt length for video:
Keep it focused. Video AI handles fewer simultaneous elements than image AI. A prompt that works for a still image might be too complex for video because the AI also needs to figure out how everything moves.
Good: "A single red rose blooming in time-lapse against a black background, soft studio lighting, macro lens, slow and elegant"
Too much: "A red rose blooming next to a glass vase on a marble table with sunlight coming through lace curtains while a butterfly lands on the petals and a cat watches from the background"
One main subject. One main action. One clear setting. That's your target for each clip.

Lesson 3: Choosing the Right Style
The visual style of your video ad communicates as much as the content itself. A polished cinematic look says "premium." A raw, handheld style says "authentic." Choose deliberately.
Cinematic / Film look
Characteristics: Wide aspect ratio, shallow depth of field, color grading, deliberate camera movements. When to use: Product launches, brand campaigns, anything meant to feel premium or aspirational. Prompt keywords: "Cinematic, film grain, anamorphic lens, color graded, shallow depth of field, dramatic lighting"
Lifestyle / Editorial
Characteristics: Natural light, real-world settings, candid feel, warm tones. When to use: Products in everyday context, relatable brand messaging, social media content. Prompt keywords: "Lifestyle photography style, natural light, candid, warm tones, editorial, everyday setting"
Product / Commercial
Characteristics: Clean backgrounds, controlled lighting, focus entirely on the product, smooth motion. When to use: Product ads, e-commerce, feature showcases. Prompt keywords: "Product commercial, studio lighting, clean background, smooth rotation, professional, high-end"
Motion Graphics / Abstract
Characteristics: Geometric shapes, typography, brand colors, animated elements, no real-world footage. When to use: Tech companies, SaaS products, explainer content, brand intros. Prompt keywords: "Motion graphics, abstract, geometric, fluid animation, brand colors [specify], modern, clean"
UGC (User-Generated Content) style
Characteristics: Looks like someone filmed it on their phone. Slightly imperfect. Authentic. When to use: Social media ads, especially on TikTok and Instagram, where polished content is often ignored and authentic content gets engagement. Prompt keywords: "Handheld camera, natural, unpolished, authentic, phone camera quality, casual"
This is a growing trend. Sometimes looking less professional performs better on social platforms because it feels real instead of corporate.
Animated / Illustrated
Characteristics: 2D or 3D animation, cartoon or stylized characters, illustrated environments. When to use: Explainers, brands with a playful identity, content for younger audiences, or when you want to avoid the issues with AI-generated human faces. Prompt keywords: "2D animation, illustrated, Pixar style, flat design animation, colorful, playful"
How to decide:
Look at your brand guidelines. Look at what your competitors are doing (and decide whether to match or differentiate). Look at the platform. TikTok rewards authentic and fast. LinkedIn rewards polished and professional. YouTube is a mix depending on your audience.
Then look at your content. A customer testimonial concept works best in lifestyle or UGC style. A new product reveal works best in cinematic or commercial style. An educational ad works best in motion graphics or animated style.
Match the style to the message, the brand, and the platform. When all three align, the ad feels natural and the viewer pays attention.

Lesson 4: Adding Voiceover and Music
AI generates silent video. Sound is where you turn a visual clip into an actual ad that moves people emotionally.
Voiceover:
You have three options:
Record it yourself. If you're comfortable on mic, this is the most authentic option. Use a quiet room, a decent microphone (even a good headset works), and speak naturally. Edit out mistakes in Audacity (free) or any audio editor.
AI voiceover tools. Tools like ElevenLabs, PlayHT, and LOVO generate realistic voiceovers from text. Type your script, choose a voice, adjust speed and tone, and export. The quality has improved dramatically. For many business applications, AI voiceover is indistinguishable from a real recording.
Best practice: Write your script first, time it against your video, then generate the voiceover. Not the other way around. Your voice should match the visual pacing.
Hire a voice actor. For high-stakes campaigns, a professional voice actor on Fiverr or Voices.com costs $50 to $200 and delivers a level of nuance that AI voiceover still can't fully replicate.
Music:
Music sets the emotional foundation. The right track can make a mediocre video feel professional. The wrong track can ruin a great one.
Royalty-free music libraries:
Epidemic Sound (subscription, huge library, great quality)
Artlist (subscription, popular with video creators)
Pixabay Music (free, decent selection)
YouTube Audio Library (free, good for YouTube content)
AI-generated music: Tools like Suno and Udio can generate custom music from text descriptions. "Upbeat corporate background music, 120 BPM, positive, modern" gives you a usable track in seconds. The quality varies, but for background music in ads, it's often perfectly adequate.
Music selection tips:
Match energy to content. Upbeat for positive messages. Subtle and ambient for serious or professional content.
Don't let music compete with voiceover. If you have narration, keep music low and instrumental. No vocals in the background track.
Start strong. The first beat of music should hit right as your ad begins. Dead silence at the start of an ad is a missed opportunity.
Consider your platform. Ads on muted platforms (many people watch Instagram and Facebook with sound off) should work visually without relying on audio. Ads on YouTube (where sound is typically on) can lean more heavily on voice and music.
Sound effects:
Subtle sound effects add professionalism. A whoosh during a transition. A soft click when a product feature appears. A notification sound to draw attention. Don't overdo it, but a few well-placed effects make a video feel polished.
Free sound effect libraries: Freesound.org, Pixabay, ZapSplat.
The audio layering order:
Voiceover (loudest, most important)
Music (background, supports the mood)
Sound effects (accents, subtle)
Most video editors let you control volume levels for each layer independently. Start with music at about 20 to 30% volume relative to your voiceover.

Lesson 2: Motion Control Explained Simply
Motion control is what separates a random AI video clip from a clip that looks like it was planned and directed. It's the difference between "AI made this" and "a professional made this."
What is motion control?
In traditional filmmaking, the director tells the camera operator exactly how to move. Pan left slowly. Push in on the product. Track alongside the person walking. Crane up to reveal the cityscape. These deliberate camera movements guide the viewer's eye and create emotion.
Kling's motion control lets you give these same instructions to the AI. Instead of hoping the AI chooses a good camera movement, you specify it.
The basic controls:
Camera movement paths. You define the camera's trajectory through the scene. Common options include:
Push in: Camera moves toward the subject. Creates focus and intimacy. Great for product reveals and emotional moments.
Pull out: Camera moves away from the subject. Reveals context and environment. Good for establishing shots.
Pan left/right: Camera turns horizontally. Good for scanning a scene or following motion.
Tilt up/down: Camera angles vertically. Tilt up creates a sense of grandeur. Tilt down creates a sense of discovery.
Orbit: Camera circles around the subject. Fantastic for product showcases.
Static: No camera movement at all. Subject moves within a fixed frame. Clean and focused.
Motion intensity. How much and how fast things move. Low intensity gives you subtle, gentle motion. High intensity creates dramatic, dynamic clips. For most business content, low to medium intensity looks more professional.
How to use motion control effectively:
Rule 1: One movement per clip.
Don't combine a pan, a tilt, and a push-in all in one 5-second clip. Pick one camera movement and do it well. This is the same rule professional cinematographers follow.
Rule 2: Match movement to purpose.
Every camera movement communicates something:
Push in = "Pay attention to this"
Pull out = "Look at the bigger picture"
Slow orbit = "Admire this from every angle"
Pan across = "There's more to see here"
Tilt up = "This is impressive or aspirational"
Static = "Focus on what's happening, not where we're going"
Choose the movement that supports your message, not just the one that looks cool.
Rule 3: Slow is almost always better.
Fast camera movements in AI video tend to introduce artifacts, blur, and incoherence. Slow, deliberate movements look cleaner and more professional. Unless your ad specifically needs high energy, keep the motion gentle.
Rule 4: Combine motion control with strong prompts.
Motion control handles the camera. Your text prompt handles everything else. A great workflow looks like this:
Write your scene description: "A luxury watch on a dark marble surface, dramatic side lighting, reflections on the surface, high-end product photography"
Set your camera motion: Slow orbit around the watch
Set intensity: Low to medium
Generate
The prompt creates the scene. The motion control creates the cinematography.
Practical example workflow:
Let's say you're creating a product showcase for a new pair of headphones.
Shot 1: "Headphones resting on a clean white surface, soft studio lighting, minimalist" + Slow push in. This draws the viewer's attention to the product.
Shot 2: "Close-up of headphone ear cushion, showing texture and material quality, macro detail" + Static shot with subtle subject movement. This highlights craftsmanship.
Shot 3: "Headphones floating against a dark gradient background, dramatic rim lighting, premium feel" + Slow orbit. This creates the hero shot.
Shot 4: "Person wearing headphones, eyes closed, enjoying music, warm natural light, lifestyle" + Slow pull out revealing the setting. This shows the product in context.
Four shots, four deliberate camera choices, one cohesive product video.
Common motion control mistakes:
Using fast, complex camera movements that cause visual artifacts
Choosing motion that contradicts the mood (a frantic pan for a calm, luxury brand)
Overcomplicating a shot that would work better as a simple static frame
Forgetting that the viewer needs time to understand what they're looking at before the camera moves

Chapter 4: Complete Video Workflow
Lesson 1: Script to Prompt to Generate to Edit
This is where everything comes together. A real AI video project isn't about one tool or one generation. It's a pipeline. Each stage feeds into the next. Here's the complete workflow from idea to finished video.
Stage 1: Script
Every professional video starts with a script, even a short one. For a 30-second ad, your script might be 5 sentences. For a 2-minute explainer, it might be a full page. The point is to know exactly what you're communicating before you create a single visual.
Write your script with timing in mind:
Average speaking pace: about 130 to 150 words per minute
A 30-second voiceover is roughly 65 to 75 words
A 60-second voiceover is roughly 130 to 150 words
Read your script out loud with a timer. If it runs long, cut it. Tight scripts make better videos.
A basic script format for AI video:
Time
Voiceover/Text
Visual Description
0-3s
"Tired of messy project tracking?"
Frustrated person looking at cluttered screen
3-8s
"Meet TaskFlow. Your projects, organized."
Clean product interface, smooth animation
8-15s
"Drag, drop, done. It's that simple."
Screen recording of the product in action
15-20s
"Join 10,000 teams who switched."
Happy team in modern office, collaborative
20-25s
"Start free today."
Logo, CTA, website URL

This table is your production blueprint. Every second is accounted for. Every visual has a purpose.
Stage 2: Prompt
Convert each visual description into an AI video prompt. This is where your prompting skills from Chapter 2 come in.
Take "Frustrated person looking at cluttered screen" and turn it into: "A professional sitting at a desk, rubbing their temples in frustration, laptop screen glowing in front of them, dim office lighting, shallow depth of field, handheld camera style, relatable and authentic mood."
Do this for every shot that will be AI-generated. Some shots might be screen recordings, real footage, or stock video instead. That's fine. Most professional AI videos mix sources.
Stage 3: Generate
Generate each clip. For every shot, create at least 3 to 4 variations. You want options. A clip that looks great on its own might not cut well with the clips before and after it. Having alternatives gives you flexibility in the edit.
Organize your files as you go. Name them clearly: "Shot1_frustrated_v1," "Shot1_frustrated_v2," and so on. When you have 20 or more clips, organization saves you from chaos in the editing stage.
Stage 4: Edit
Bring everything into a video editor. For most people, CapCut (free, beginner-friendly) is the best starting point. DaVinci Resolve (free, more powerful) is the step up. Adobe Premiere Pro is the industry standard if you have a subscription.
The editing process:
Import all clips, voiceover, and music into your editor.
Lay down the voiceover or text timing first. This is your backbone.
Place video clips to match the voiceover timing.
Trim clips so transitions feel natural. Cut on motion or beats for smoother flow.
Add text overlays, titles, and CTAs.
Add music and sound effects.
Adjust audio levels (voiceover loudest, music underneath, effects as accents).
Color correct if needed to make all clips feel visually cohesive.
Watch the full video 3 times. Fix anything that feels off.
Export.
The truth about editing:
Editing is where amateur AI videos become professional ones. Two people can generate identical clips, but the one who edits better will produce a dramatically superior final product. Editing skills are worth developing even if they feel separate from AI. They multiply the value of everything you generate.

Lesson 2: Adding Captions and Subtitles
Captions are not optional anymore. They're a core part of video content.
Why captions matter:
85% of Facebook video is watched without sound. On Instagram and TikTok, the number varies but silent viewing is the default behavior for many users. If your video only works with audio, you're losing most of your audience.
Beyond silent viewers, captions improve accessibility for deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences, help non-native speakers follow along, and increase watch time because viewers who read along stay engaged longer.
Types of captions:
Burned-in captions (hardcoded). These are permanently part of the video. They show up regardless of platform settings. This is the standard for social media content. You've seen these: the bold, animated words that appear on screen as someone speaks. They're baked into the video file.
Subtitle files (SRT/VTT). Separate text files that platforms overlay on the video. YouTube and LinkedIn use these. The viewer can toggle them on and off. Use these for longer-form content, YouTube videos, and platform-hosted content.
How to add captions:
Auto-captioning tools. CapCut has built-in auto-captioning that transcribes your voiceover and places text on screen automatically. It's fast and surprisingly accurate. Descript also does this well, and lets you edit the video by editing the text transcript.
AI transcription. If your editor doesn't have auto-captioning, use a tool like Whisper (free, by OpenAI), Otter.ai, or Rev to generate a transcript. Then import the timestamps into your editor.
Manual captioning. For short videos (under 30 seconds), sometimes it's fastest to just type the captions manually in your editor and time them by hand.
Caption style for social media:
The trend on platforms like TikTok and Instagram is bold, animated captions. Keywords get highlighted in a contrasting color. Words appear one or two at a time, synced to the voiceover. This style keeps eyes on the screen.
Practical settings that work:
Font: Bold sans-serif (Montserrat, Roboto, or the platform defaults in CapCut)
Size: Large enough to read on a phone screen. Bigger than you think.
Position: Center or lower third of the screen. Avoid the very bottom where platform UI might overlap.
Color: White text with a dark outline or shadow for readability on any background
Highlight color: One accent color for key words (your brand color works well)
Caption tips:
Keep each caption block to 1 to 5 words. Walls of text on screen are hard to read.
Time your captions to match natural speech rhythm. Each phrase should appear exactly when it's spoken, not before or after.
If you're not using voiceover and instead have text-only narration, the captions ARE your narration. Time them to a reading pace that feels natural, not rushed.
Always proofread auto-generated captions. They make mistakes with names, technical terms, and numbers.

Lesson 3: Music and Sound Effects
We covered music selection in Chapter 2, so this lesson focuses on the practical side: how to actually work with audio in your video projects.
Finding the right music:
Start by identifying the emotion you want. Not the genre. The emotion.
Confident and forward-moving? Look for tracks with a steady beat, building progression, and major key melodies.
Calm and trustworthy? Ambient pads, soft piano, minimal percussion.
Urgent and exciting? Faster tempo, driving drums, rising tension.
Playful and light? Acoustic guitar, ukulele, light electronic beats.
Go to your music library (Epidemic Sound, Artlist, Pixabay Music, or YouTube Audio Library) and search by mood rather than genre. Most libraries have mood filters. Use them.
Working with music in your editor:
Match cuts to beats. This is the single most impactful audio editing technique. When you transition between clips, align the cut with a beat in the music. This creates a subconscious sense of rhythm that makes the video feel polished. In your editor, zoom into the audio waveform. The peaks are the beats. Place your cuts there.
Volume automation. Your music shouldn't be the same volume throughout. It should duck under voiceover and rise during visual-only moments. In most editors, you can add keyframes to the audio track to raise and lower volume at specific points. A typical pattern:
Intro (no voiceover): Music at 70-100%
During voiceover: Music drops to 15-25%
Transition moments: Music briefly rises to 40-50%
Outro/CTA: Music returns to 70-100%
Sound effects that elevate your video:
You don't need many. Three to five well-placed sound effects in a 30-second video make a big difference.
Useful sound effects for business videos:
Whoosh: For transitions between scenes
Click or tap: When a UI element appears or a button is highlighted
Subtle impact/thud: When a product lands on screen or a key message appears
Rise/swell: Building tension before a reveal
Notification ping: Drawing attention to a feature or callout
Where to find them: Freesound.org (free, huge library), Pixabay (free), ZapSplat (free with account), or the built-in effect libraries in CapCut.
Common audio mistakes:
Music too loud over voiceover. If someone has to strain to hear the narration, your music is too loud. Always err on the side of quieter music.
No audio at all. Silent videos feel empty and unfinished even on platforms where many viewers watch muted. The viewers who do have sound on will notice.
Music that doesn't match the visual mood. Upbeat pop music over a serious, professional brand video creates cognitive dissonance.
Abrupt audio endings. Fade your music out over the last 1 to 2 seconds instead of cutting it dead. In your editor, add a short audio fade at the end.
Ignoring audio quality for voiceover. A great script delivered with bad audio (echo, background noise, low volume) undermines everything. Record in a quiet space. Use noise removal tools if needed (Audacity has a free one).

Lesson 4: Exporting for Different Platforms
You've scripted, prompted, generated, edited, captioned, and sound-designed your video. Now you need to get it out into the world. Exporting incorrectly can ruin all that work. A beautiful video that's blurry, cropped wrong, or laggy on playback wastes your effort.
Universal export settings:
For nearly all social media platforms, these settings work:
Codec: H.264 (the universal standard) or H.265 (smaller files, same quality, but less compatible with older devices)
Resolution: 1080p minimum. 4K if your source material supports it and the platform accepts it.
Frame rate: 30fps for most content. 24fps for a cinematic feel. 60fps for smooth motion (sports, fast action).
Bitrate: 10-20 Mbps for 1080p. Higher is better quality but larger file size. For social media, 10-12 Mbps is the sweet spot.
Platform-specific requirements:
This is where it gets practical. Each platform has its own preferences for aspect ratio, length, and file size.
Instagram Reels:
Aspect ratio: 9:16 (vertical)
Resolution: 1080 x 1920
Max length: 90 seconds
Format: MP4
Tip: Keep key content away from the top and bottom 15% of the frame. Platform UI overlaps those areas.
TikTok:
Aspect ratio: 9:16 (vertical)
Resolution: 1080 x 1920
Max length: 10 minutes (but 15-60 seconds performs best)
Format: MP4
Tip: Front-load your hook. TikTok's algorithm weighs early watch-through rate heavily.
YouTube (standard video):
Aspect ratio: 16:9 (horizontal)
Resolution: 1920 x 1080 (minimum), 3840 x 2160 (4K preferred)
Max length: 12 hours (but match length to content purpose)
Format: MP4
Tip: Upload the highest quality file you can. YouTube re-compresses everything, so starting with higher quality means the final result looks better.
YouTube Shorts:
Aspect ratio: 9:16 (vertical)
Resolution: 1080 x 1920
Max length: 60 seconds
Format: MP4
Tip: Add a strong hook in the first 2 seconds. Shorts loop automatically, so make the ending flow back into the beginning if possible.
LinkedIn:
Aspect ratio: 16:9, 1:1, or 9:16 (all supported, but 1:1 and 16:9 are most common)
Resolution: 1920 x 1080 for landscape, 1080 x 1080 for square
Max length: 10 minutes (but 30 seconds to 2 minutes performs best)
Format: MP4
Tip: LinkedIn auto-plays on mute. Your first frame and captions need to carry the video without sound.
Facebook:
Aspect ratio: 16:9 or 1:1 for feed, 9:16 for Stories/Reels
Resolution: 1080p minimum
Max length: 240 minutes (but under 1 minute for ads)
Format: MP4
Tip: Square (1:1) takes up more screen real estate in the feed than landscape, which can improve engagement.
Twitter/X:
Aspect ratio: 16:9 or 1:1
Resolution: 1920 x 1080
Max length: 2 minutes 20 seconds (140 seconds)
Format: MP4
Tip: Shorter is better here. Aim for under 45 seconds.

Lesson 5: YouTube, Instagram, TikTok Formats
Understanding format isn't just about pixel dimensions. Each platform has a different culture, audience behavior, and content expectation. The same video edited three different ways can succeed on all three platforms. The same video posted identically across all three usually underperforms on at least two of them.
YouTube: The long game
YouTube rewards depth. Even Shorts are often entry points to longer content. The platform's algorithm favors watch time, click-through rate, and session time (keeping people on YouTube).
For AI-generated video on YouTube:
Longer content works. Explainers, tutorials, product deep-dives, compilations.
Thumbnails matter enormously. Use AI image generation to create compelling thumbnails. High contrast, readable text, expressive faces or bold visuals.
Descriptions and tags are searchable. YouTube is the world's second-largest search engine. Optimize your video description with relevant keywords.
End screens and cards drive viewers to more of your content. Plan your video's ending to direct viewers to a next action.
AI video workflow for YouTube: Generate B-roll and visual sequences with AI. Combine with screen recordings, talking-head footage (real or AI avatar), and voiceover. YouTube content typically mixes sources rather than being 100% AI-generated.
Instagram: The visual showcase
Instagram rewards aesthetics and immediacy. Content needs to look good in the first frame because that's the thumbnail. The algorithm favors saves, shares, and watch-through rates on Reels.
For AI-generated video on Instagram:
Reels (9:16 vertical) get the most reach. This is where Instagram pushes new content to non-followers.
First 1 to 2 seconds determine whether someone stops scrolling. Make them count with a striking visual or bold text hook.
Polished but not corporate. Instagram users respond to content that feels curated but human. Overly slick corporate video gets scrolled past.
Use carousel posts for educational content. This isn't video, but it's worth noting because carousels consistently outperform single images on Instagram.
Stories are for connection and behind-the-scenes. Less polished, more personal.
AI video workflow for Instagram: Generate short, visually striking clips. Edit them tight with captions and trending audio. Focus on one message per Reel. Post consistently rather than occasionally posting masterpieces.
TikTok: The authenticity engine
TikTok rewards attention and relatability. The algorithm is the most democratic of any platform. It tests your content with small audiences first and scales distribution based on performance, regardless of your follower count.
For AI-generated video on TikTok:
Raw often beats polished. The most successful TikTok content looks like someone made it in their bedroom, even when it's carefully planned.
Hooks are everything. You have about 1 second before someone swipes. Start with a question, a bold claim, a visual surprise, or text that creates curiosity.
Trends move fast. If you use AI to create trend-based content, speed matters. A trend that's hot today may be dead in a week.
Sound matters more here than on any other platform. TikTok is a sound-on platform. Audio choice can make or break your video's reach.
Comment engagement affects distribution. Create content that makes people want to comment. Ask questions. Make debatable claims. Leave intentional gaps for viewers to fill in.
AI video workflow for TikTok: Generate quick, punchy clips. Keep it short (15-30 seconds sweet spot). Use native TikTok editing features and sounds when possible because the algorithm may favor natively edited content. Don't overthink production value. Think about what makes someone stop, watch, and share.
Repurposing across platforms:
The efficient approach is to create your core content for one primary platform, then adapt it for others.
If your primary is YouTube (long-form):
Extract the best 30-60 second segment for Instagram Reels
Grab the hook or most surprising moment for TikTok
Create a square cut for LinkedIn
If your primary is TikTok (short-form):
Compile related TikToks into a longer YouTube video
Repost directly to Instagram Reels (but native creation usually performs better)
Add professional context and post on LinkedIn
The key is adapting, not just reposting. Change the aspect ratio. Adjust the pacing. Modify captions for each platform's culture. A few extra minutes of adaptation dramatically improves performance compared to posting the identical file everywhere.
